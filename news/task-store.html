Nadia works on a payment service. It is 6 pm on a Friday. In her editor, the assistant has just fixed a test in three exchanges, and she is about to close the laptop. One thing has been waiting for two weeks: forty configuration files to migrate to a new format. It is not hard. It is long and repetitive, and she wants neither to watch it happen nor to find it half done on Monday in a lost conversation.

Friday, 6 pm: the request that does not fit in a conversation

She types her request the way she would say it to a colleague. One word changes everything: “launch”. She is not asking for an explanation or a fix in the open window; she is asking for a piece of work to be taken on, run, and reported back later.

  1. You
    Launch the migration of the 40 config files to the new format, and tell me tomorrow what was done and what got stuck.
  2. Assistant
    Explicit execution request: I am preparing a task card rather than working here.
    Title: “Migration of the 40 configuration files”. Workflow: automatic@1.0.0. Mode: Act.
    Your prompt stays in a private file, outside the repository and the Memory Bank.
    Before any launch, I check that this host exposes an authenticated launch action. If it does not, I stop and tell you: no partial card, no launch announced without an acknowledgement.

The answer is deliberately cautious, and that is by design. The /aikido-launch-task command is used only when the person explicitly asks to launch or execute a task. It collects a private prompt, a title, the installed agent and the mode, then looks for the host’s real capability. Without a launch adapter, it stops and says so. AI-KIDO launches nothing itself: it starts no resident process and makes no network request.

Prompt or card: what tips the balance

The problem this rule avoids is simple: turning every exchange into a tracked task would make the assistant heavy and bureaucratic. The rule fits in one sentence: ordinary conversation, analysis or implementation requests stay in the current conversation unless the person asks for a task card. “Fix this test”, “explain this function”, “add a field to the form”: all of that stays a prompt, and Nadia made three such requests this afternoon without a single card appearing.

The card comes when the request carries one of three explicit intents: launch a piece of work now, schedule it for a date, or turn it into a plan to instantiate. “Launch the migration of the 40 files tonight and tell me tomorrow” ticks the first. “Prepare a plan to split the migration, I will approve each step” ticks the third: the /aikido-plan-task command saves the approved plan to a private file, creates it against a versioned workflow, shows it with its ID, version and hash, and only after authorization instantiates it as cards linked in a cycle-free graph.

Following by prompt: what Nadia reads on Saturday morning

Saturday, coffee in hand, Nadia reopens her terminal. She wants to change nothing, only to know where things stand. The /aikido-show-tasks command is made for that: it lists or inspects cards without mutating their state. A read touches neither the revision nor the timestamps, and the output stays limited to her workspace. Her private prompt does not appear in a shared response.

What she reads is each card’s state and its revision. If the migration produced a result, the card is in review: a result, whether ok, partial, blocked or error, moves the card to review or waiting, never to accepted or done. If a card is waiting, aikido task resume changes it back to ready, provided she passes the revision she just read; after a rejection, an explicit acknowledgement is also required. Resuming launches nothing: the authenticated host authorizes and dispatches.

Following in the directory: what the files say

Nadia could also look at where these cards live. By default the store sits outside the checkout, in the operating system’s user-data directory, under aikido/tasks/<workspace-id>. The Task Store rejects a path inside the workspace, rejects symlink components in the path, and rejects any write without its exclusive lock. None of it goes into Git, the Memory Bank or Kimono.

The journal is the authority: the projections, that is the views of the cards, can always be rebuilt from it. The store.json file carries the store schema; it is how indexing, search and package generation recognise a Task Store root and exclude it. The rule is never to edit these files by hand, and never to let a model choose or apply a state transition.

To check the store’s health, Nadia types aikido task store doctor. Here is what lies under the cards: the only raw output in this article, from a still-empty store, identifiers removed.

What a card keeps, and what it refuses

On Sunday the migration ran. Thirty-eight files went through; two got stuck on an unexpected format. The temptation would be to say “done”. The card refuses to say it on its own: the result is partial, the card enters review, and it stays there until Nadia decides.

The problem avoided here is work declared finished by whoever did it. The rule: acceptance is an explicit, authorized decision separate from the result, and “done” is a further transition that requires acceptance and verified criteria. Concretely, Nadia accepts the card by stating her decision and her reason; the two stubborn files become a new attempt on the same card, never a replacement card. If the card has required children, it cannot be done before them.

And if she had said “tonight at 10 pm”?

The scheduled version of the same request goes through /aikido-schedule-task. It requires a future date with its offset and an explicit IANA timezone; an ambiguous or past time is rejected rather than silently converted. The Task Store then records an intent, nothing more: the card carries a registration status that starts at “intent recorded”. The authenticated host requests the schedule, reports it through a requested, acknowledged, unavailable or error event, and keeps the job ID. A date does not wake a closed editor, and aikido task run --due only previews the due cards.

The vision: cards you look at, not only cards you query

Everything above exists today in AI-KIDO’s public files: the commands, the private store, the journal, the states and the transitions. What follows is the direction we are taking, not a shipped feature. We want cards to be read the way one reads a board, without typing a command.

  • History and state of playLook back at what was asked, by whom, when, with which revision and which decision; and, at a glance, the cards that are ready, running, waiting with their reason, in review, done.
  • View by workflowThe automatic path on one side, the person-defined workflows on the other, each card at its step.
  • View by agent and by task natureWhich agent or persona was wanted, which one actually worked; and group cards by what they do: analysis, implementation, migration.
  • DashboardParents and children, dates, possible resume, and only the actions allowed by the current transition.

The vision rests on a rule we already enforce in shipped code: a view never renames a state, it triggers an authorized transition. Dragging a card from one column to another will have to pass the same revision check as aikido task transition does today. And whatever the view, no card enters the search corpus or a shared export.

Try it at home

  1. Keep ordinary requests in the conversation: fix a test, explain a function, add a field.
  2. When a piece of work must outlive the open window, say so explicitly: “launch”, “schedule for”, “prepare a plan”.
  3. The next day, type /aikido-show-tasks and read the state and revision before any action.
  4. Accept what was done yourself, then mark the card done: two gestures, not one.
  5. Check the store with aikido task store doctor; never open its files to edit them.

On Monday, Nadia found a card in review, two files to redo and a journal that says exactly what happened. Nothing in Git, nothing in the Memory Bank, nothing forgotten.
